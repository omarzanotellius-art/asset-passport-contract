Questions you might have

What does the blockchain add over a signed, dated PDF?

Three narrow things. Nobody, including whoever runs these pages, can quietly change or remove what is recorded. The date each entry was recorded is set by the network, so a history filled in just before a sale shows as such. And the record does not depend on any one party: it moves with the asset when it is sold. It does not prove an entry is true, or that nothing is missing.

Why would an O&M provider record its work here?

That is the main open question, and this demo does not answer it: its entries were written by a script. One plausible reason is that a dated record of its own work, which nobody can change, protects a provider when there is a dispute over who caused a failure. In real use, the owner would decide which wording is public.

Isn't the second farm's story staged?

Yes, deliberately, so the idea is visible in two minutes. Real histories are messier, which is part of the case for recording work as it happens rather than reconstructing it later.

This is a demonstration, not a product or service offering. It runs on Polygon Amoy, a public test network that may be retired; a full copy of the record is kept in the project's public repository.

The location is real; the farms, companies, people and events are fictional. Recorded dates shown in the passports are simulated; the blockchain explorer shows when each entry was actually written. A few data-page details are illustrative and not recorded on the network; each passport's key explains which.

In real use, the owner would choose, entry by entry, which wording is public. Everything else would stay as a fingerprint, readable in full only by people the owner authorizes. Anything published on a blockchain is permanent and cannot be removed.
